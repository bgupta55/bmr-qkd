Security-rate trade-off in quantum key distribution: From individual links to large-scale networks
Stefano Pirandola ,1,* Cillian Harney,2 and Gaetana Spedalieri2
1Department of Computer Science, University of York, York YO10 5GH, United Kingdom
2nodeQ, 71–75 Shelton Street, Covent Garden, London WC2H 9JQ, United Kingdom
(Received 31 December 2025; accepted 23 June 2026; published 24 August 2026)
We explore the interplay between security and key rate of a quantum key distribution (QKD) protocol.
This fundamental trade-off is investigated both at the link layer, i.e., for a QKD protocol implemented on a
channel, and at the network layer, i.e., within the context of a multiuser multihop communication network. In
the former scenario, we consider both discrete- and continuous-variable protocols in the composable ﬁnite-size
framework, showing how their epsilon security restricts the key rate achievable on a single ﬁber link under
various assumptions for the process of parameter estimation. In the latter scenario, we analyze the impact of
composable security in the network setting, studying the interplay between end-to-end (ETE) epsilon security
and ETE key rate for arbitrarily chosen remote end points. Starting from basic topologies to more complex
ones, such as perturbed lattices, we analyze this trade-off under different types of routing strategies. In multipath
routing, a greater number of paths can improve key rates, but this enhancement is accompanied by a reduction in
the ETE security level. In the setting of single-path routing, we introduce a modiﬁed Dijkstra algorithm, which
we call “lambda protocol,” able to optimize route construction depending on the joint ETE demand for security
and rate. Our results are ﬁrst derived for speciﬁc network instances (i.e., ﬁxed topologies within a given network
class) and subsequently extended to ensemble averages over entire network classes, where phase transitions are
revealed with respect to node connectivity.

I. INTRODUCTION
It is known that the performance of quantum key distribu-
tion (QKD) [ 1–4] is limited by a fundamental rate-loss scal-
ing, exactly identiﬁed by the Pirandola-Laurenza-Ottaviani-
Banchi (PLOB) repeaterless bound [ 5]. More precisely, the
PLOB bound represents the secret-key capacity of the bosonic
lossy channel, i.e., the maximum amount of secret bits per
channel use that can be distributed over a lossy communi-
cation line, such as an optical ﬁber: a result later extended
to free-space channel [ 6] and a satellite connection [ 7]. It is
known that the introduction of intermediate relays or repeaters
(e.g., trusted nodes, twin-ﬁeld nodes [ 8], and other types of
nodes [ 9–12]) can overcome this bound and extend the dis-
tance of QKD. For this reason, it is crucial to develop multihop
QKD networks, where the distance limitation is mitigated by
a more gentle rate-loss scaling that depends not only on the
number of repeaters in a chain between two end points but also
on the type of routing considered. See Ref. [ 13] for the max-
imum QKD rates (capacities) that are achievable for end-to-
end (ETE) communications in an arbitrary network topology.
Besides the rate-distance trade-off, another fundamental
trade-off is between rate and security. This appears naturally
in the context of composably secure QKD, where secret key

rates are guaranteed only up to an error probability, called “ep-
silon security” ε. The latter represents the overall probability
of failure of the QKD protocol due to practical imperfections
affecting parameter estimation (PE), error correction (EC),
and privacy ampliﬁcation (PA). Each of these processes has an
associated error probability: ε
PE for PE, the “correctness” εcor
for EC, and the “secrecy” εsec for PA. The epsilon security is
the sum of these values ε≃ εPE + εcor + εsec and bounds the
overall probability that Alice’s and Bob’s keys fail to be equal
and/or private. It is widely accepted as the overall security
level of the protocol.
The inﬂuence of epsilon security on the rate of a QKD
protocol is widely acknowledged within the community. How-
ever, a clear and comprehensive analysis of its impact on
both individual links and larger networks remains absent. In
this work, we investigate these issues in a range of scenarios,
encompassing discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable
(CV) protocols, from single ﬁber links to multihop communi-
cation networks.
For a QKD protocol implemented over a single link, we
begin by analyzing the direct impact of epsilon security
on the key rate, stemming from the composable terms that
appear in the standard block-based QKD rate expressions.
We then extend this analysis to session-based QKD, where
multiple blocks are exchanged over a stable channel and pa-
rameter estimation is performed on the entire set of session
points. This extension highlights the trade-off between the
increased key rate enabled by improved estimators and the
reduced security resulting from the accumulation of errors
across multiple blocks, demonstrating that data processing
plays a nontrivial role in shaping the balance between rate and
security.

Most interestingly, we analyze the impact of composable
security in QKD networks, from basic topologies to more
complex ones, such as Waxman random meshes and perturbed
lattices. In particular, we investigate how the choice of the
routing strategy can heavily shift the balance between ETE
security level and rate performance. In the setting of multipath
routing, it is crucial to evaluate the beneﬁt of a generally
increased key rate versus a reduced security level induced
by the epsilon composition over the greater number of links
associated with the paths. In the context of single-path routing,
the trade-off is played at the level of individual path construc-
tion, which may be optimized over a joint security-rate cost
function. For this purpose, we introduce a suitably modiﬁed
Dijkstra algorithm, where a lambda parameter can be set
to continuously shift the priority between security and rate.
The fundamental trade-off between security and rate is ﬁrst
illustrated through examples of network topologies, and then
analyzed across entire classes of networks using ensemble
averaging. Phase transitions are examined in relation to node
connectivity.
The paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we discuss the
security-rate trade-off for QKD protocols over a single link.
In Sec. III, we analyze the trade-off in the context of basic
QKD networks, extending the investigation to more complex
topologies in Sec. IV. In Sec. V, we extend our analysis to
authentication. Section VI is for conclusions.

QKD network, i.e., a network with a stable topology, where
the properties of the links and the trusted nodes are constant

notation and assumptions.

C. Perturbed lattice-based networks
An interesting class that is often considered for assessing
network performance is lattices. These are networks with un-
derlying graphical models that obey consistent connectivity
patterns, and when embedded into a geometrical space form
a regular tiling or tessellation. They are useful due to their
ease of description, symmetries, and expedience of analyti-

often used as a sandbox for network protocol benchmarking.

as connectivity/nodal density and ETE capacities [ 32]. This
model utilized inﬁnite graphs with close to degree-regular

The giant component of a network N
gc is the largest subset
of nodes between which all nodes possess paths to one an-
other. The giant-component fraction (GCF) is thus the ratio
between the size of the giant component N
gc and the number
of nodes N deﬁned to belong to the network. The GCF pro-

and its ability to establish communication between any/all
parties.
In the context of quantum networks, the GCF is a very
useful metric that is tightly coupled to the quality of point-to-
point communication links across the network. If a network is
sparse, nodes that should be connected in the ideal lattice may
become very far apart. The QKD link connecting these nodes
may be of insufﬁcient quality to be established (zero rate, or
below some threshold acceptable rate). Consequently, connec-
tions will fail during network generation, and the resulting
topology will not be fully connected. This phenomenon leads
to percolative behavior with respect to network connectivity,
i.e., phase transitions between poorly connected and fully
connected topologies.

Our previous results for single links and networks can
be extended to include authentication. Suppose that, on a
link, we run an ε

secure authentication scheme that is iterated a times per round
(block) of the QKD session (with nbks blocks). Then, follow-
ing Ref. [ 35], the overall epsilon security of the session is
bounded by nbks (aεauth + εblock ).
For the ﬁrst QKD block, the parties need an initial
preshared authentication key of length Kinit = bhash + abtag,
where bhash is the number of bits needed to select a uni-
versal hash function (hash function key bits) and btag ≃
⌈log2 (1/εauth )⌉ is the bit length of the tag used in the Wegman-
Carter authentication of each of the a authentication messages
[36]. Using key recycling [ 35], the initial bhash bits are re-
cycled in later blocks (and sessions), while the abtag bits are
consumed as fresh one-time-pad material for the authentica-
tion tags. After the ﬁrst block, fresh tag keys can be drawn
from the QKD output of previous blocks.
The impact on the key size can therefore be estimated as
follows. Assume that a single block produces a secret key
length of l bits. Then, thanks to key recycling [ 35], this would
be reduced to l → l − ab

VI. CONCLUSIONS
We have investigated the fundamental trade-off between
composable security and key rate in QKD at both the link
and network layers. For individual links, our analysis has
examined the interplay between the security level and the
achievable key rate for DV - and CV -QKD protocols, such as
the BB84 and the Gaussian-modulated protocols, assuming
various strategies for the crucial process of parameter esti-
mation. We have examined how enforcing a given security
level impacts the key rate of these protocols, both for single-
block processing and for multiblock session processing (more
suitable for practical implementations).
We have then extended our analysis to the framework of
multihop QKD networks, starting from basic topologies to
more complex conﬁgurations (Waxman meshes, perturbed lat-
tices). In this setting, we have analyzed the interplay between
security and rate for ETE connections, which are randomly
selected within the network. We have quantiﬁed how ETE
security degrades with the use of multipath routing strategies,
despite the associated rate gains. For single-path routing, the
“lambda protocol,” a security-rate-aware modiﬁcation of Di-
jkstra’s algorithm, enables route selection that balances these
competing demands. This protocol can be tuned to be security
optimized or rate optimized in extreme cases.
Our investigation addresses two levels of ETE metrics:
average ETE metrics, obtained by averaging over all ETE
connections within a single network, and ensemble-average
ETE metrics, obtained by further averaging across networks
belonging to the same class. In the latter case, we demonstrate
that giant-component transitions, occurring at speciﬁc critical
nodal densities, can trigger a sharp decoupling between secu-
rity and rate in the trade-off. The magnitude of this behavior
depends on several factors, including the QKD protocol im-
plemented at the link layer and the type of lattice assumed for
the network.
In conclusion, our results provide a uniﬁed framework for
understanding and optimizing the fundamental security-rate
trade-off in QKD networks, bridging the gap between link-
level protocol design and large-scale network architecture.

APPENDIX C: ETE RA TE FROM LINK RA TES
An ETE key is generated by successively swapping keys
along a path using one-time-pad sessions. When multipath
routing is available, the ETE keys obtained from parallel paths
are concatenated, resulting in a larger composite key. Key
swapping and composition will occur at every time T agreed
by all the trusted parties. Under stationary conditions, the
key-swapping interval T can be assumed to be much longer
than the typical time required to complete a QKD session
on an individual link ( T ≫ n
bksN/rs). Consequently, many
sessions are performed and many key blocks are generated
within each interval. Under this assumption, truncation effects
arising from unequal session or block lengths across different
links can be neglected, and the session end points can be
treated as synchronized across all links. For this reason, the
(maximum) length of the key string generated on each link
can be estimated as the product of the corresponding link key
rate (in bits per second) and the total duration T .
Having established this general context, we now discuss
how the ETE key rate between two end points is computed
from the key rates of the individual links along the path(s)
connecting them. The resulting ETE key generation rate is not
always obvious to determine: It may be exact or expressed as
an average, depending on whether the protocols operating on
the individual links are deterministic or probabilistic.
1. Deterministic link protocols
For simplicity, consider two adjacent links e1 = (a, c) and
e2 = (c, b) with associated key rates R1 = Re1 and R2 = Re2
(the reasoning can be implicitly extended to multiple links).

those rates, have unit success probabilities, i.e., p1 = p2 = 1.

value R = min{R
